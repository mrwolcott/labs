Oscillation Analysis

Measure how fast something is swinging back and forth, from your own video. Nothing leaves your device.

1

Load your clip

Choose the video file of your twang.

checking the file

Set the first box to the camera mode you filmed in. Slow motion is usually 120 or 240. Normal video is 30 or 60. The app fills in the second box by itself by watching the clip play, so leave that one alone unless it fails.

2

Measure the swing

Start here. Move the slider to a moment before the twang, where the ruler is sitting still. Mark the edge it is clamped against, then mark its tip, and type in the overhang you measured. The ruler acts as its own measuring stick, so there is nothing to hold up to the camera and nothing sitting at the wrong distance.

These two marks do three jobs. The tip gives the app the still position to measure every swing from. The line between them gives the direction the ruler points, so the swing is measured square to it even if the ruler sits at an angle in the picture. And that same line is a length you already know, which turns pixels into millimetres.

Do this one first, and only once. Click the object in every single frame, working forward through the clip. Use the arrow keys to step and click to place each point. It is slow, but it is the only measurement here that assumes nothing at all about how the ruler moves: you are recording where it actually was, frame by frame, and letting the shape appear on its own.

Watch the graph as you go. Once you have a couple of swings down you will be able to see what shape the motion has, and that shape is what makes the quicker method on the next tab trustworthy.

This uses the marks and the overhang from the Set the scale tab as well.

This uses the marks and the overhang from the Set the scale tab.

This is the quick way, and it works because of what the trace showed you. If the motion is a smooth swing that repeats, then the only moments you actually need are the ones where it turns around. Everything in between is already accounted for by the shape. So instead of sixty clicks you need about a dozen, and you can afford to do this for every overhang.

Find a frame where the object is as far over as it gets, then click on the object. Do that for every turning point you can find. You do not have to stick to one side: the app works out which side each click is on and handles it. Ten or more gives a good result, and it fits a line through your clicks so one slightly late click barely matters.

If you could hear a clear note when you twanged it, that pitch is much higher than the number you are about to measure, and both are right. A bar held at one end also bends in faster patterns at the same time, and those are the ones that carry as sound, while the big slow swing you are measuring barely moves any air.
